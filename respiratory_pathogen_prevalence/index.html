How the numbers were built

Each pathogen starts from its share of illnesses with an identified cause in a typical 2015–2019 year. These starting shares come from etiology studies: the CDC EPIC pneumonia studies for the lower tract, and common-cold, pharyngitis and ear-infection studies for the upper tract.

Each calendar year, that share is scaled by how active the pathogen was compared with a typical year. Virus activity comes from CDC lab surveillance (NREVSS). Bacteria and fungi use national case counts for pertussis, Legionnaires’ disease, tuberculosis and Valley fever, plus published surge reports. Influenza and SARS-CoV-2 use CDC’s estimates of symptomatic illnesses directly. The values are then rescaled so each tract adds to 100%.

What a share means

A share is the fraction of symptomatic illnesses with a known cause. It is not test positivity, and it is not deaths. Illnesses with no identified pathogen are left out, which is roughly a quarter of colds and over half of adult pneumonias. A co-infection is counted once, under its main pathogen.

Upper tract: colds, sore throat, sinusitis, ear infections and croup (ICD-10 J00–J06, H65–H66). Lower tract: bronchiolitis, acute bronchitis and pneumonia (J09–J22).

Illness counts: each share is turned into a number of illnesses by anchoring it to CDC’s influenza estimates. Every flu illness counts once in the upper tract, and 15% of them count in the lower tract, so one share point of each tract stands for a fixed number of illnesses. That comes to about 1 identified-cause upper tract illness per US resident per year and about 30 million lower tract illnesses. Population figures are Census Bureau estimates. Counts are rounded to the nearest 1,000.

These are modeled estimates. The large categories are uncertain by a few percentage points. The 2026 values cover January to September, and the July–September portion partly relies on assumptions.
